Why there are two different measurements

The pipeline reports volume — count the enhancing voxels, watch that number across visits, call the response from how much it changed. That decision was made on 2026-07-21, choosing volumetric RANO over the classic 2D method.

But every expert rating in this dataset was made by a radiologist with a ruler, writing things like Target L.: 29mm x 38mm. So we are building a better instrument and the only answer key we have was written with the old one.

Picture building a digital kitchen scale where the only recipe book you can test against measures in cups. You will never report in cups. But when your scale says 240 grams and the book says two cups, you need to know whether you two agree.

There is a second, stronger reason. RANO will only let you track a lesion as a target if it is at least 10 mm across and still 10 mm across turned ninety degrees. That rule is written in millimetres, not cubic millimetres — and we proved separately that no volume threshold can stand in for it (min_absolute_change_mm3 turned out never to change a single call).

So: volume is what we report. The ruler is two tools we cannot do without — a translator for reading the expert's answer key, and the gate that decides which lesions are allowed into the answer at all.
Decided

A mask says which voxels are tumour, not how many tumours

The segmentation labels every voxel. It is completely silent on how many separate lesions those voxels form — and nothing in a label map encodes that. You have to impose it.

The rule is: two voxels belong to the same lesion if they touch. The only question is what "touch" means.

26 is permissive and merges things that barely touch. 6 is strict and shatters a thin enhancing rim into fragments. We swept both across all 93 practice-arm timepoints, at every volume floor from 0 to 100 mm³.

The argument that settled it was one neither of us expected. At a 20 mm³ floor there are timepoints where 6-connectivity ends up with fewer lesions than 26. Connectivity can only ever split things, never merge them — so the only way that happens is that 6-connectivity breaks a real lesion into pieces that each fall under the floor and get thrown away.

Decision: 26-connectivity, 20 mm³ floor. The floor is anchored, not tuned — 5 to 10× below anything that could satisfy the 10×10 mm bar, and about the size of a single native voxel column on a 5 mm acquisition. It costs 0.192% of the enhancing volume in the whole arm. Full evidence in the lesion-splitting memo.
The trap that comes with the floor. At 20 mm³, seven timepoints lose their entire lesion inventory. Those timepoints still have disease — 3 to 48 mm³ of it. An empty inventory therefore means "no measurable target lesion" and must never be read as "no disease" or allowed to produce a CR. Volumetry never applies the floor; it counts every voxel.
Learned

One lesion can look like three

Scrolling through slices, a lesion often appears as scattered specks that look like separate things. On Patient-067 at week-109, slice 84 showed the enhancing tumour as three clearly separate patches.

They are one lesion. All three belong to the same 3D object, joined through the slices above and below.

The striking part: that lesion occupies 16 slices and appears as more than one piece on all 16 of them. It is never a single blob anywhere. So a rule like "it is split if it looks split on every slice" would call it split, and be wrong.

Across the whole practice arm, 50.4% of the slices occupied by a largest lesion show it as more than one disjoint piece. This is the normal case, not an edge case — which is why a ruler may never be drawn across the gap between two pieces on a slice.

The reason for the three patches on Patient-067 turned out to be simple: it is a broken ring. The enhancing tissue is a rim around a dead centre, and the rim has gaps in it. Adding the necrosis back gives one region of 371 voxels instead of three arcs of 53, 37 and 9.

Learned

Is this the same lesion as last time?

Counting lesions on one scan is not enough. RANO needs to know whether a lesion at this visit is the same lesion as last visit — that is what makes a new lesion detectable at all.

Every LUMIERE scan is resampled into a standard atlas space, so all of a patient's visits sit on an identical 182×218×182 grid. That suggests something beautifully simple: stack the visits like sheets of tracing paper and hold them up to the light. Overlapping lesions are the same lesion. A lesion whose shadow falls on blank paper is new.

It worked beautifully on large lesions and completely failed on small ones. Which sent us looking for why, and that turned out to be the most important thing we found.

Fixed

The scans were never lined up

Same grid is not the same as aligned, and the difference had been invisible for the whole project up to this point.

Each visit was registered to the standard template independently, and those registrations disagree with each other. The existing fingerprint check verifies that every image shares one grid, and it passes — correctly. It just never compared the contents.

The control is the brain itself: a patient's brain does not wander between visits, so any mismatch is ours.

Consecutive visits' brain masks overlap only 91%, and the brain's centre of mass sits about 2.7 mm out (worst case 10.2 mm).

Three other explanations were tested and each ruled out by its own number:

Could it be…Then we would seeWe measured
inconsistent skull-strippingdifferent brain volumes volumes agree to within 1%
scans covering different amounts of headdifferent brain heights 85 of 86 pairs agree within 10 mm

Same size, same height, and the disagreement nowhere near the disease. The clincher: Patient-067's brain runs from z=26 to z=155 at week-053 and from z=17 to z=145 six weeks later — the same height to within a millimetre, sitting nine millimetres lower.

Why did this stay hidden for so long?

Everything built before this measures volume, and a voxel count does not care where the voxels are. You can count a tumour perfectly on a scan that is a centimetre out of place. The first operation that cared about position was asking whether a lesion is the same one as last time. Registration was always Piece 2 in the roadmap and explicitly flagged as a dependency for new-lesion detection — we simply arrived at it from a side door while chasing a ruler that gave the wrong answer.

A plain shift does not fix it — sliding the brains together recovers only 22% of what is missing, because the head is also tilted. The typical fit turns it by 3.3 degrees, one by nearly 18. So we added SimpleITK and built proper rigid registration: six numbers per visit, three to slide and three to turn, matched on brain minus lesion (the tumour is the thing that changed, so letting it drive the fit means pulling everything else out of true).

All 93 visits registered in 74 seconds. Brain overlap 91.0% → 95.1%, worst case 69.6% → 85.6%, and nothing made worse. The number that mattered: how much of a lesion lands on the same lesion next visit, for lesions under 500 mm³, went from 4% to 30%. Large lesions went 90% → 98%.
The check that earned its keep on day one. The first run registered three visits with an 80 mm shift and the optimiser reported convergence for all three — from the inside it looked exactly like the successful ones. Nothing but asking "did the brains actually end up better aligned?" catches that. Every fit is now judged on that outcome; failures fall back to a plain slide or are left unregistered and flagged. 3 of 93 needed it.
Open

New lesions: still unsolved, and we know why

Registration was supposed to unlock new-lesion detection. It did not, and the reason is worth writing down carefully because it rules out a whole family of fixes.

Our rule asks: does this lesion overlap anything from last time? If not, it is new. Here is where the newly-enhancing tissue actually sits, relative to the previous visit's tumour, at the visits the radiologist called new:

week-013   10,167 mm³ of new tissue     6.7 mm away    radiologist: NEW
week-055      184 mm³                   1.8 mm away    radiologist: NEW
week-073    1,401 mm³                   0.2 mm away    radiologist: NEW
week-105      783 mm³                   0.4 mm away    radiologist: NEW

week-120   56,247 mm³                  87.6 mm away    (we DID catch this one)

Read the last row against the rest. When disease appeared right across the brain, our rule caught it without difficulty. Every other time the new tissue is touching the old tumour — a fifth of a millimetre away, in one case. Something touching the old tumour overlaps it, so the rule says "same lesion", correctly by its own definition, every single time.

You cannot align your way out of two things being in contact. Registration was a blocker, not the blocker.

And distance carries no signal. Across the arm, the distance from the old tumour to the newest enhancing tissue is a median 3.5 mm when they call it a new lesion and 3.6 mm when they call it ordinary growth. Volume of new tissue is worse than useless — one visit they called plain growth had forty times more new tissue than a visit they called a new lesion.
So what are the radiologists actually doing?

Their own words give it away. Across 329 rationales they write less than 3 months 19 times, and repeatedly note whether something is within the irradiated area or outside of irradiation field. One entry reads "dural recurrence despite within 3 months of radiochemotherapy".

They are answering a question we never asked: is this enhancement even tumour? After surgery a patient gets radiotherapy, and irradiated tissue lights up on contrast exactly like tumour does. New enhancement inside the treated area, soon after treatment, is often not cancer at all. Outside the field, radiation cannot explain it — so it is real disease.

They use three things we do not have: when radiotherapy happened (roughly recoverable from week numbers), the radiation dose map (not in this dataset), and perfusion imaging (not in this dataset — LUMIERE has T1, contrast T1, T2 and FLAIR).

We approximated the field — post-operative cavity plus residual tumour, grown by a margin — and asked whether new enhancement fell inside or outside it. It did not separate them. At every margin, roughly the same proportion of "new lesion" visits and "ordinary growth" visits had tissue outside the zone. As a rule it scored 41% precision against a 31% base rate, worse than the connectivity rule it was meant to beat.

Why that attempt was doomed, in hindsight. Only 32 of 329 rationales invoke the field or the clock at all — about one in ten. We found a genuine piece of domain reasoning and over-weighted it into the main mechanism. It applies in a specific early window, and there are only seven such visits in the whole practice arm.
A cohort-lock breach, recorded rather than hidden. The first time these notes were counted, the count read every patient in the ratings file — including the 17 held-out patients the lock says must not be examined before the scoring run. That gave 39 of 501. The figures above exclude the held-out arm and come from scripts/experiments/rationale_vocabulary.py. The conclusion did not change, and every phrase quoted on this page was checked against the permitted notes alone.

One more idea did work — better than anything else we tried. Think about what each kind of change looks like. A lesion that grows puts on a thin new layer all round its edge, like a skin. A new focus arrives as a lump with an inside to it, even when it is touching the old tumour. So take only the newly-enhancing tissue and peel one millimetre off everything. A skin disappears. A lump survives.

70 assessable transitions · 22 new lesions reportedCatchesPrecision
Overlap rule — a new object touching nothing from last visit 8 of 22 (36%)50%
Overlap rule, objects above 500 mm³ only 5 of 22 (23%)5 of 5

The lump rule doubles what we catch, at about the same precision. And its extra flags — the ones the notes do not mention — are not scattered. Of the visits where the notes name no new lesion, it fires on 62% of those already called progression and on none of those called complete or partial response. The notes are terse, not an inventory, so silence is not proof of absence: these extras look like real disease that simply was not written down.

What we have. Two usable signals. The lump rule for reach — it catches three quarters of the new lesions the radiologist reported. The 500 mm³ overlap rule for certainty — five flags, five confirmed. Neither one's silence can be read as "no new lesion".
Where we stopped, and what is honest about it. The lump rule was designed and measured on the same seven patients, so it is promising, not proven. Patient-072 is still the hard case: at a 100 mm³ lump it catches 2 of its 9. The next step is to open the six visits it still misses in the browser and look at the MRI beside the mask. Either there is a visible nodule the segmentation never marked — then the ceiling is DeepBraTumIA, and no rule over this mask recovers it — or it is marked and we are failing to isolate it.
Built

Measuring a lesion

On each axial slice: the longest straight line across the lesion, then the longest line at right angles to it. Multiply. Keep the slice with the biggest product. Four decisions turned out to be load-bearing.

  1. Both lines stay inside the tissue. A caliper you could not physically draw on the image is not a measurement. Free on a normal blobby cross-section; on an L-shape the unconstrained caliper reads 42.4 mm by cutting across the empty corner instead of 31.6 mm. The overshoot is kept as a shape warning — 0.00 mm on the median real cross-section, firing on 3 of 17.
  2. One contiguous piece at a time. Half of all slices show a lesion as several islands. A ruler across two of them measures the healthy brain between.
  3. Enclosed holes filled first — and only what the tissue completely surrounds. A surgical cavity open along one side never fills, because the fill runs out of the opening. That is why this is safe and why simply adding the necrosis label is not: label 2 holds resection cavities many times larger than the tumour around them (Patient-062: 1,631 mm³ of tumour beside 73,661 mm³ of "necrosis").
  4. Lengths between voxel corners. Ten 1 mm voxels measure 10.0 mm, not the 9.0 mm you get centre-to-centre. On a 10 mm threshold, that millimetre decides measurability.

The perpendicular was wrong twice before it was tested properly — the first version returned zero on every shape — and both times the numbers it produced on real lesions looked perfectly reasonable. Plausible is not correct. So it is now checked against shapes whose answers are known before measuring: see the validation page, where every shape is drawn with the lines the code actually chose on it.

Curved shapes read about one voxel high; grid-aligned shapes read exactly right. That is true of the mask, not a fault in the ruler — a rasterised circle genuinely is bigger than the smooth circle it came from, because the voxels near its diagonal stick out past the boundary. It does not upset the 10 mm gate, because the gate needs both lines over 10 mm and the perpendicular is the less inflated: a true 10.0 mm disc passes, a 9.5 mm one does not.

Against the 18 real measurements the radiologists wrote down:

Where we measured…Result
the same lesion they did (6 of 17) long axis median +1.2 mm, perpendicular +0.5 mm
the measurability gate vs their written numbersagrees 14 / 17
a different lesion (11 of 17)all lesion-identity, none a ruler error

That +1.2 mm is the same one voxel the synthetic shapes predicted. The phantoms told us what to expect and the real data agreed — which is the strongest evidence we have that the ruler is sound.

Every remaining disagreement is about which lesion, not how it was measured. Two causes, both already familiar. We merge lesions they keep apart — Patient-031 week-190, where the radiologist wrote three separate measurements and our 26-connectivity produced one 46,000 mm³ object measuring 82×45. And we point at the wrong lesion — the biggest one, while they follow a target chosen at an earlier visit and demoted when it shrank. Neither is fixed by a better ruler.

What is solid, what is open

The one open problem, stated plainly

Two separate failures turned out to be the same problem wearing different hats: we do not know which lesion to point at.

Follow Patient-072 and you can watch a radiologist work. At week-013 they pick a lesion and measure it: 19×12. It grows — 26×19, then 36.5×18.5. At week-037 it shrinks by more than half and they write "Target L. to non-target L." — they demote it and stop measuring. Two visits later something new appears and they promote it: 13×11.5. From then on that is the one they follow.

The old lesion is still sitting there, still the biggest enhancing object in the scan. They walk past it every visit. So the target lesion is not the biggest, or the roundest, or the brightest — it is whichever one was chosen earlier and has not been demoted yet. That is a piece of history, not a piece of geometry, and you cannot recover it from one scan.

Standing rules that are easy to forget and expensive to break. Volumetry never applies the lesion floor. An empty lesion inventory means "no measurable target", never CR. Post-Op and Pre-Op scans are not assessable timepoints — comparing lesions across a surgery is meaningless, and the cohort lock says so. The held-out arm (17 patients) is never inspected. The expert ratings are one neuroradiologist's reading, not a consensus — we are fitting one person's judgement, and RANO reproducibility between readers is a known weak point in the field.

If you only remember four things

1. A mask tells you which voxels are tumour. It never tells you how many tumours, or which one is the same as last time. Everything hard lives in that gap.

2. Same grid is not the same as aligned. Volume is blind to position, so this hid for the whole project until the first question that cared about position.

3. Test geometry against shapes whose answers you know before measuring them. Plausible numbers on real data hide broken code — twice, here.

4. Do not trust a fit because the optimiser says it converged. Check the outcome it was supposed to improve. Three 80 mm registration failures reported success.
